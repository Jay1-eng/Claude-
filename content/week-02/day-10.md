### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
Somewhere near you, a vet clinic, a dental office and an air-conditioning company have the same name they've had for twenty years, the same staff, the same sign. And at some point in the last few years, each of them was quietly sold to an investment fund you've never heard of. Nobody announced it. The receptionist might not even know. This is the roll-up — the strategy private equity has used to buy up tens of thousands of boring local businesses — and it explains a surprising amount about why your pet's check-up, your filling and your furnace repair all cost more than they used to.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
For most of history, these businesses were un-investable. A vet practice or a plumbing company was one owner, one building, maybe a dozen staff. Too small for Wall Street to care about, too personal to run from a distance, and impossible to scale — you can't franchise a root canal. So they stayed family-owned. The owner ran it for thirty years, then sold it to a younger partner for a modest price, or just closed. That was the deal. Then investors noticed three things: these businesses have customers who can't shop around, demand that doesn't fall in a recession, and owners who are getting old with nobody to sell to. That last part is the whole opportunity.

**[1:30 — The playbook]**
Here's how a roll-up works, step by step. Step one: a private equity fund buys one solid business — call it a vet practice doing a million dollars a year in profit. That's the "platform." Step two: it goes shopping. It buys ten, twenty, fifty more practices in the region, each one a retiring owner happy to get a cheque. These are the "bolt-ons." Step three: it centralises the boring parts — purchasing, billing, HR, marketing, software — and leaves the sign and the vet in place so the customer sees nothing. Step four: it raises prices, adds services and pushes volume. Step five, a few years later: it sells the whole thing to a bigger fund, or takes it public, and starts again. In vet care, Mars — the candy company — did this at a global scale, acquiring chains until it owned around three thousand hospitals. In dentistry, "dental support organisations" backed by funds now run the business side for well over ten percent of American dentists. In heating, plumbing and air conditioning, dozens of fund-backed platforms have each bought hundreds of family contractors in a few years. Same playbook, three industries.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Now the part that makes it so profitable, and it's a trick of arithmetic called multiple arbitrage. A small business is valued as a multiple of its profit. A one-owner clinic might sell for three to five times its annual profit — say four. A million in profit, four million price. But a large, professionally run chain of fifty clinics is valued very differently. It's bigger, more predictable, easier to sell, so buyers pay ten, twelve, sometimes fifteen times profit. So watch what happens. The fund buys fifty clinics at four times. It staples them together. The combined company is now valued at twelve times. The same clinics, the same profit — tripled in value, on paper, just by being owned together. That's before the fund improves anything. Then add the operational squeeze: better prices from suppliers, one billing team instead of fifty, and — the big one — pricing. A family vet charges what feels fair in their town. A platform charges what the data says the market will bear, and it adjusts every quarter. In heating and air conditioning, the shift is even more visible: technicians are increasingly paid on commission, and the "free inspection" that ends with a recommendation to replace the whole unit is a well-documented pattern. The business didn't get worse at fixing furnaces. It got better at selling them.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
Who pays for the arbitrage? Three groups. First, customers. Veterinary prices have risen faster than general inflation for years, and regulators have noticed: the UK's competition authority opened a full investigation into the vet market, and US agencies have put healthcare roll-ups under scrutiny. Second, the staff. Vets and dentists report pressure to hit revenue targets, and burnout in veterinary medicine is a documented crisis. The founder who cared about the town is replaced by a regional manager who cares about the dashboard. Third, sometimes, the fund itself. Roll-ups are built with debt. When interest rates jumped in 2022 and 2023, several heavily borrowed platforms in dentistry and healthcare ran into trouble, and some have gone through restructurings. A strategy that works beautifully with cheap money gets dangerous when the money gets expensive. And there's a quieter cost: when every clinic in a region is owned by two funds, the "shop around" option disappears, and with it the thing that kept prices honest.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What does this mean for you? As a customer: ask who owns the business. If the clinic or contractor is part of a platform, get a second quote on anything expensive — the independent down the road is often cheaper precisely because nobody is arbitraging its multiple. As a business owner: this is why your boring business is worth more than you think. A fund will pay a real price for a plumbing company with clean books and recurring customers, and knowing the playbook is the difference between selling well and being bought cheaply. My take: the roll-up is one of the most effective money-making machines in modern finance, and almost none of its gains come from making the service better. That's not a scandal. It's just worth knowing before you pay the bill.
