### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
In April 2015, Amazon did something it had refused to do for nine years. It showed investors the numbers for a division most of its customers had never heard of. Wall Street expected a money-losing experiment. Instead, that division had made a billion and a half dollars in a single quarter — and it was profitable. Amazon's stock jumped about fourteen percent in a day. The division was Amazon Web Services, and it turned out to be the most profitable thing Amazon had ever built. Today it brings in roughly one dollar in six of Amazon's revenue… and, in a typical year, well over half of its operating profit. This is the story of how a side project became the company.

**[0:40 — The assumption]**
Ask anyone what Amazon is and they'll say: the everything store. Boxes on the porch. Prime. Same-day delivery. And that's real — Amazon's online store sells hundreds of billions of dollars' worth of goods a year. But here's the thing about selling goods: it's a terrible way to make money. You buy the item, you warehouse it, you ship it, you handle returns, and you compete with every other seller on price. For most of its history, Amazon's retail business ran at margins of a few cents on the dollar — and some years it lost money outright. Investors tolerated it because the revenue kept growing. What they didn't know was that, inside the company, a completely different business had quietly been built.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
Rewind to the early 2000s. Amazon's engineers had a problem: every new project meant rebuilding the same plumbing — servers, storage, databases. So they standardised it. Internal teams could request computing power the way you'd order from a menu. Then someone asked the obvious question: if this is useful to us, would other companies pay for it? In 2006 Amazon launched two products almost nobody noticed. S3 — rent storage by the gigabyte. And EC2 — rent a server by the hour. No contracts, no hardware, a credit card and a few clicks. For startups, this was a revolution. Before AWS, launching a web company meant buying racks of servers up front. After AWS, you could start for a few dollars a month and scale only when customers showed up. Netflix moved its entire streaming operation onto it. Airbnb, Slack, and thousands of startups were born on it. Even the CIA reportedly signed a six-hundred-million-dollar deal in 2013. Jeff Bezos later said AWS enjoyed a head start of about seven years before serious competition arrived. In business, seven years is forever.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
So why does cloud print money when retail doesn't? Three reasons. One: scale economics. Amazon buys servers, electricity and bandwidth in quantities nobody else can match, so each unit of computing costs it less than it would cost you. Two: utilisation. A company running its own servers sizes them for the busiest hour of the year and leaves them idle the rest of the time. AWS packs thousands of customers onto the same hardware, so the machines are always busy — and always earning. Three: stickiness. Once your data, your software and your staff's skills live inside AWS, leaving is painful and expensive. Customers stay for years and spend more every year. Now the numbers. In 2023 AWS brought in about ninety-one billion dollars of revenue — roughly sixteen percent of Amazon's total — but around twenty-five billion of operating profit, out of Amazon's thirty-seven billion. Two-thirds of the profit from one-sixth of the sales. In 2024 the gap narrowed as retail improved, but AWS still delivered close to forty billion of about sixty-nine billion. And AWS has done this while cutting prices more than a hundred times since launch — because each cut brings in more customers, which drives more scale, which lowers costs again. It's the same loop Costco runs with memberships, except the product is the internet's plumbing.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
It isn't free money, and there are three catches. First, the bill. Amazon is now spending unprecedented sums on data centres — capital spending ran to tens of billions in 2024 and was guided even higher for 2025, much of it for AI computing. If demand for AI doesn't keep up, those are very expensive empty buildings. Second, competition finally arrived. Microsoft's Azure and Google Cloud have been growing faster than AWS for several years, and AWS's market share has slipped from well over a third of the market to roughly thirty percent, according to industry trackers. Third, the awkward part: Amazon rents servers to companies it competes with. Retailers, streaming services, logistics firms — many of them pay their biggest rival for the privilege of running their business. Some, like Walmart, have refused on principle. And looming over everything: energy. Data centres are becoming one of the fastest-growing consumers of electricity on the planet, and regulators have noticed.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
Here's what this means for you. First, when you hear "Amazon had a bad quarter," check which Amazon. The store can stumble while the cloud carries the company. Second, almost everything you do online — stream a show, book a stay, send a message — probably runs on servers owned by three companies, and Amazon is the biggest. You're an AWS customer whether you shop at Amazon or not. My take: the everything store is a brilliant, loss-tolerant magnet for customers and data, and AWS is the business that pays for the magnet. Amazon didn't win retail. It won the plumbing underneath everyone else's business — and that's a far better place to be.
