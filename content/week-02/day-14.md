### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
One of the best-known investors in mobile home parks once described his business like this: it's like a Waffle House where the customers are chained to their booths. He meant it as a compliment to the business model. A lot of people heard it as a confession. Both readings are correct, and that tension is the whole story. Today: why some of the largest investment firms in the world have spent the last decade buying trailer parks, why the numbers are among the best in all of real estate, and why this is the one boring business on this channel where "boring" and "uncomfortable" sit side by side.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
Say "trailer park" and most people picture something run-down, temporary, maybe a little chaotic. The reality is different. Around twenty million Americans live in manufactured homes — factory-built houses delivered to a plot and set on a foundation. For many, it is the only form of home ownership they can afford: a decent two-bedroom might cost a fraction of a conventional house. And the key word is ownership. In most parks, the resident owns the home. They just don't own the land it sits on. They rent that — the lot, the road, the water and sewer line — from whoever owns the park. For decades, that was usually a local family who'd owned the land since the sixties. Investors didn't look twice. Then a handful of people did the maths.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
The model got noticed in stages. In the nineties, a Chicago investor named Sam Zell — one of the sharpest real-estate minds of his generation — started buying parks and eventually built a public company with hundreds of them. Then, in the 2000s, a pair of operators began buying small parks across the country and, crucially, teaching others to do it, through bootcamps and a well-known newsletter. By the 2010s the big money arrived: private equity firms and pension-backed funds bought portfolios of parks by the thousand. Why? Because each one had three things investors almost never find together: steady demand that doesn't fall in a recession, costs that the tenants mostly carry themselves, and a customer base that, as we're about to see, effectively can't leave.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Here's how the money works. The resident pays lot rent — typically somewhere between three and six hundred dollars a month, more in expensive states. Compare that to an apartment, and it sounds cheap. Now look at it from the landlord's side. In an apartment building, the landlord owns the walls, the roof, the boiler, the appliances — and fixes all of them. In a park, the landlord owns dirt, pavement and pipes. The home is the resident's. If the water heater breaks, it's the resident's water heater. So the landlord's operating costs run far lower than an apartment's — industry figures put expenses at roughly a third of rent, versus half or more for apartments. That's the first lever. The second lever is immobility. Despite the name, mobile homes don't move. Moving one costs five to fifteen thousand dollars, often more than the home is worth, and many parks won't accept a home older than a few decades. So when a new owner raises lot rent by fifty dollars, the resident does the maths: pay six hundred a year more, or spend ten thousand to leave — if anyone will take the home at all. Almost everyone stays. Turnover in a well-run park is close to zero. The third lever is supply. Almost no town in America will approve a new mobile home park; the neighbours object, the zoning says no. Meanwhile existing parks get closed and redeveloped. So the supply of parks is fixed or shrinking, while demand for cheap housing keeps rising. Fixed supply, captive customers, low costs. That's not a real-estate play. That's a utility. And like a utility, the income is astonishingly predictable: a hundred-lot park at four hundred dollars a lot is forty thousand dollars a month that arrives whether the economy is booming or not, because the alternative for the resident is homelessness, not a nicer apartment across town. And investors have paid accordingly — parks that once sold for cheap now trade at prices that rival apartment buildings.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
Now the uncomfortable part. First, the ethics. The residents are, on average, among the lowest-income homeowners in the country — retirees on fixed incomes, working families, people for whom a hundred-dollar rent increase is real hardship. The model's best feature from an investor's view — nobody can leave — is its worst from a resident's. Rent hikes after a sale have been reported across the country, and the backlash has been loud: investigative reporting, a famous late-night segment, and lawsuits. Second, politics. Several states have responded with rent-increase limits for parks, mandatory notice periods, and laws giving residents a right of first refusal to buy their park when it's sold. Resident-owned cooperatives, where tenants buy the land together, now number in the hundreds. The regulatory risk here is real and growing. Third, the pipes. Many parks were built sixty years ago with private water and sewer systems that are now failing. A single infrastructure repair can wipe out years of profit, and it's the one cost that lands squarely on the landlord. And finally, the reputation. A business whose profits come from people who can't leave will always be one headline away from a crisis. Some funds have quietly started to pull back for exactly that reason. There's also a slower, structural risk: the homes themselves age. A park full of forty-year-old homes has residents who can't sell, can't move and can't afford to replace, and a landlord who eventually inherits the problem when a lot goes empty and no newer home will fit.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What does this mean for you? If you're looking at investments, understand what you're actually buying: not a park, but a captive customer base, and everything that comes with it. If you're a resident or know one, the single most important question is who owns the land and what the lease says about rent increases. My take: as a pure business model, this is one of the most elegant things in real estate — fixed supply, low costs, zero churn. As a way to treat people, it depends entirely on the owner, and the incentives push the wrong way. Brilliant business. Uncomfortable business. I'd rather you knew both halves.
