### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
Outside almost every gas station this time of year sits a rack of firewood bundles. Six, seven dollars each. Each bundle is a few sticks — less than a cubic foot of wood. Now do the arithmetic nobody does at the pump: a full cord of firewood is a hundred and twenty-eight cubic feet. At bundle prices, that cord is worth over a thousand dollars. The same cord, delivered loose to a driveway, sells for about three hundred. And the log it came from may have cost nothing at all. So why isn't everyone with a chainsaw rich? Today: the real economics of firewood, from free log to kiln-dried bundle, and the four reasons this business almost never grows beyond one person and a truck.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
Firewood looks like the easiest business in the world. Trees are everywhere. The tool is a chainsaw. People will always want to be warm. And the margins, on paper, are absurd: input cost near zero, output price in the hundreds. In rural America it's a classic side business — the farmer, the logger, the guy with forty acres and a splitter who sells cords off a hand-painted sign. That image is accurate. It's also exactly why the business stays small, which we'll get to. First, how it really starts.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
Most firewood operations begin as a by-product. A tree service clears a lot and has to pay to dispose of the logs, so it dumps them at someone's yard for free. A farmer thins a woodlot. A landowner cleans up after a storm. Suddenly there's a pile of hardwood and a winter coming. The first sales are to neighbours. Then word spreads, a bigger splitter arrives, and the owner starts buying log-length loads from loggers — a tractor-trailer of oak, maple or ash, around fifteen hundred to two thousand dollars, which yields eight to ten cords once it's cut and split. That's a wood cost of maybe a hundred and fifty to two hundred dollars a cord, against a sale price of three hundred or more. The margin looks great. Then the labour starts.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Here's the machine, and the whole thing is throughput. Step one: buck the logs to sixteen-inch rounds with a chainsaw. Step two: split them — by hand with a hydraulic splitter, two to three thousand dollars, at roughly a cord a day for one person. Or with a firewood processor, a machine that cuts and splits in one pass, which costs twenty thousand to well over a hundred and turns out one to three cords an hour. Step three, the step that kills beginners: wait. Green wood has to season for six to twelve months until the moisture falls below about twenty percent, or it won't burn properly and the customer won't come back. That means the wood you sell this winter was cut last winter, and the money you spent on logs sat in a pile for a year. Step four: sell. There are three markets. The driveway cord, delivered, two hundred fifty to four hundred fifty dollars. The campground and gas-station bundle, where a kiln-dried, shrink-wrapped three-quarters of a cubic foot sells for six or seven dollars — a thousand-plus per cord, minus the kiln, the wrap, the labour and the retailer's cut. And the restaurant and pizza-oven market, which pays a premium for specific species, dried and delivered on schedule. The kiln is what separates a side business from a real one. Kiln-drying takes days instead of months, kills pests, and unlocks the bundle and restaurant markets. It also costs tens of thousands of dollars and a lot of fuel — so the operator who buys one has just turned a free-log business into a capital-intensive one.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
Now, why does it never get big? Four reasons. One: wood is heavy and cheap. A cord weighs two to three tons and sells for three hundred dollars. Trucking it fifty miles eats the profit, so every firewood business is trapped inside a radius of about thirty miles. No national firewood company can exist, because the product can't travel. Two: the cash cycle. You pay for logs and labour a year before you sell, so growth means financing a bigger pile of wood that isn't earning anything yet. Three: the labour. Even with a processor, firewood is lifting, stacking, loading and delivering tons of material by hand in the cold. It's hard to hire for and harder to keep. Four: the bugs. Because firewood spreads invasive pests like the emerald ash borer, states restrict moving it across lines, and selling bundles in retail chains requires federal heat-treatment certification and inspection. The compliance isn't impossible, but it's one more thing a one-person operation rarely does. Put those together and you get an industry made almost entirely of local operators doing a few hundred cords a year, with a handful of bundle companies running kilns near big markets. The big players sell convenience at a gas station, not cords in a driveway.

**[7:00 — Could you do this?]**
So could you? At the small end, yes, and it's one of the more honest winter side businesses on this channel: a splitter, a trailer, a source of logs, and the patience to cut this year's wood for next year's customers. The people who do well pick one of two lanes. Either they own the local market — the thirty-mile radius, the repeat customers, the restaurant accounts — and never try to leave it. Or they go up the value ladder with a kiln and sell bundles and specialty wood where the price per cord quadruples. What doesn't work is the middle: buying expensive equipment to sell cheap cords. My take: firewood is the clearest lesson on this channel that a great margin and a great business are different things. The margin is in the wood. The business is in the thirty miles around your pile.
