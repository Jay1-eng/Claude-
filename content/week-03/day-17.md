### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
Search for a storage unit online and you'll see a price that looks almost too good: eighty-nine dollars a month for a ten-by-ten. Sign up, move your stuff in, and six months later the bill says a hundred and thirty-nine. Nothing broke. Nothing changed. You just discovered how the self-storage industry actually makes money — and why, in a market where the advertised price for a new customer has been falling for two years, the biggest companies are still doing fine. This is the story of a boring business that got too popular, the building spree that followed, and the pricing machine that decides who survives the hangover.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
We covered the basics in an earlier Short: around fifty thousand facilities in America, more than the big three fast-food chains combined, operating margins that can top seventy percent once a building is full, and tenants who put a padlock on a door and rarely come back. For a decade, self-storage was the quiet darling of real estate. It outperformed apartments, offices and shopping centres through the 2008 crash, because the demand comes from what the industry bluntly calls the four Ds — death, divorce, dislocation and downsizing — and none of those stop in a recession. So the belief hardened: storage is recession-proof, it's simple, and it always fills up. The first two are mostly true. The third one is where the money got lost.

**[1:30 — The boom]**
Then came 2020. Millions of people moved, built home offices, left cities, moved back in with family, or just needed somewhere to put the exercise bike. Storage demand didn't rise; it spiked. Occupancy at the big chains pushed past ninety-five percent, and asking rents in 2021 and early 2022 hit records — in some markets up twenty, thirty percent year on year. The public companies posted the best numbers in their history, and the biggest deal in the sector's history followed, a twelve-billion-dollar merger between two of the top chains. Developers looked at all this and did what developers do. Land was bought, plans were drawn, loans were signed at the lowest interest rates in living memory. The problem is that a storage facility takes two or three years to design, permit and build. So the buildings approved at the peak of demand in 2021 and 2022 all opened in 2023, 2024 and 2025 — tens of millions of square feet a year, concentrated in the fast-growing Sunbelt cities where everyone had the same idea. In some of those metros, the storage pipeline grew faster than the population it was meant to serve.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
To understand who wins from here, you need the pricing machine, because it's the whole game. Big storage operators run two prices. The first is the street rate — the number on the website, the one that lures a new customer. In an oversupplied market, that number has to fall, and it has: national asking rents have declined year over year since 2023, by double digits in the most overbuilt cities. If that were the only price, the industry would be in real trouble. But there's a second price: what existing customers pay. Once your belongings are inside a unit, you are, in the industry's own language, sticky. Moving out means a truck, a weekend and a new padlock, over a few dollars a month. So the operators raise in-place rents aggressively — regular, data-driven increases, often well above inflation — and most customers absorb them. Analysts call it ECRI, existing-customer rate increases, and it's the reason the big chains can advertise eighty-nine dollars while their average tenant pays far more. The spread between the teaser and the in-place rent is where the profit lives. It's the gym model and the airline-miles model again: the customer who stays funds the discount that lures the next one. And this machine runs on scale. A chain with thousands of properties has pricing software, national marketing, and a brand people search for. A single-site owner has a sign by the road and whatever the chain down the street decides to charge this week.

**[5:30 — The bust]**
So who's actually hurting? Not the giants. Their buildings are largely paid for, their customers are sticky, and when a stressed new facility comes up for sale, they're the buyers. The pain is concentrated in three places. First, the newly built. A facility that opened in 2023 in an overbuilt metro is trying to fill up — a two-to-four-year job in normal times — while cutting rents to compete with five other new buildings doing the same thing, and paying interest on a loan that may have reset to twice its original rate. Second, the developers who were counting on selling to the big chains at peak prices; those buyers are now paying less and waiting longer. Third, and this is the one nobody saw coming: the housing market. Storage demand is tied to moving, and high mortgage rates froze moving. Existing-home sales fell to their lowest levels in decades. People who don't move don't need storage. The four Ds still happen, but the fifth D — dislocation by choice — quietly disappeared. Put those together and you get a sector where the business model still works, the incumbents still profit, and a wave of recent entrants is stuck in a slow, expensive lease-up with no exit in sight.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What should you take from this? If you're a customer: that teaser rate is bait. Budget for the increases, and remember you can call and negotiate — operators would rather keep you at a smaller bump than lose you to the new building next door. If you're thinking about the business: the lesson isn't "storage is bad." It's that a great boring business attracts a crowd, and the crowd arrives all at once. The time to build was when nobody was talking about it. My take: self-storage remains one of the best models in real estate for the people who already own it, and one of the worst places right now to be the newest building on the block. Same industry. Opposite outcomes. The difference is timing and scale.
