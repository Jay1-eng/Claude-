### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
Tonight, around fifteen hundred stores across America will do their biggest day of the year. Tomorrow morning most of them will start taking down the shelves, and within a couple of weeks they'll be gone — the windows papered over, the lease ended, the staff laid off until next August. No going-out-of-business sale. No failure. That was the plan. Halloween pop-ups are the most visible example of a business model that most people never think about: the temporary store. And once you understand why it works, you'll see it everywhere — fireworks tents in June, Christmas tree lots in December, tax offices in March. This is the business of showing up, selling hard, and leaving.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
Normal retail assumes permanence. You sign a five- or ten-year lease, you build out the store, you hire a year-round staff, and you pray that enough people walk in every month to cover the rent. That model has a weakness: demand isn't flat. For most products, a huge share of the year's sales lands in a few weeks — Halloween, Christmas, back-to-school, tax season. A permanent store carries twelve months of rent to capture six weeks of demand. The pop-up flips that. It only pays for the weeks that matter. The question is where you find a landlord willing to rent for eight weeks — and the answer is the second half of the story.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
In 1983, a man running a struggling women's clothing shop in California noticed a costume store across the street doing enormous business every October. He tried a Halloween section, then a temporary Halloween store, and the temporary version worked better than the permanent one ever had. The idea grew slowly, then a mall gift-shop chain — the one you've walked past a thousand times — bought it in the late nineties and turned it into a machine. Every year the chain scouts thousands of vacant retail boxes, signs short leases for the ones in the right spots, and ships in costumes, animatronics and shelving by the truckload. Opening day is in August. Closing day is November first. Repeat. Today it runs around fifteen hundred stores a season and reportedly sells well over a billion dollars of Halloween a year — in spaces that, for the other ten months, nobody wants.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Here is the machine, and it has three gears. Gear one: vacancy arbitrage. America has far more retail space than it needs — dead malls, former supermarkets, empty big-box stores. A landlord with a vacant box earns zero. A pop-up offers a short lease, often at a steep discount or a percentage of sales, and the landlord takes it, because something beats nothing. The pop-up gets prime square footage for a fraction of the permanent rate, and the landlord gets a lit, trafficked store that makes the rest of the centre look alive. Gear two: concentrated demand. Americans spend over eleven billion dollars on Halloween, and the spending curve is a spike, not a line. A store that exists only inside that spike captures most of the revenue with none of the off-season cost. Seasonal staff, no year-round payroll. Gear three: inventory recycling. The stock is bought in bulk from the same suppliers every year; what doesn't sell gets discounted hard in the final days, and what still doesn't sell goes back to a warehouse for next season. Costumes don't rot. Put the gears together and the economics look nothing like a normal store: rent for eight weeks, labour for ten, inventory that carries over, and a sales density per week that a permanent store would kill for. The same three gears drive the fireworks tent that appears in the supermarket car park every June, the Christmas tree lot on the corner, and the tax-prep storefront that opens in January and closes in April. Different product, identical model. And there's a fourth, quieter gear: the brand does the marketing for free. Nobody needs an ad to know Halloween is coming. The calendar is the campaign, the orange banner is the store locator, and the same customers come back every year because the date never moves. A permanent retailer spends a fortune reminding you it exists. A pop-up just has to be there when you already know you need it.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
Now the catch, because a business that earns its whole year in eight weeks has the same weakness as a farmer. First, concentration. One bad season — a hurricane week, a recession October, a pandemic — is not a bad quarter. It's a bad year, and there's no second harvest. Second, inventory. If you guessed wrong on what people want, you don't have months to discount your way out. You have days. The unsold stock is either stored at your cost or dumped. Third, the lease itself. Short leases are cheap because they're uncertain: the landlord can rent the box to a permanent tenant and leave you without a store in August, and the best locations get competitive. Fourth, labour. Hiring, training and then releasing hundreds of people every year is a grind, and in a tight labour market it gets harder. And one more that's easy to miss: the model depends on vacancy. If retail space ever gets scarce again, the pop-up's cheap rent disappears with it. That's why the biggest chain has started experimenting with a second season — Christmas pop-ups in the same boxes — to stretch the lease and the staff across more of the year. It's a tell: even the best operator in the model knows eight weeks is a narrow place to stand.

**[7:00 — Could you do this?]**
So could you run a pop-up? At the small end, yes, and people do every year. A Christmas tree lot is a two-month lease on a corner, wholesale trees, a fence and a cash box. A fireworks tent is often run on consignment from a distributor — they supply the stock, you supply the labour and the permit, and you split the sales. A costume or gift pop-up in a local mall is a short lease and a van full of inventory. The rules are the same at every size: pick a demand spike, find a landlord with an empty space, keep the inventory returnable or recyclable, and plan to make nothing for ten months. My take: the pop-up is the most honest bet in retail. There's no slow decline, no pretending — you go all in, sell for eight weeks, and find out. For the right person that's terrifying. For the right person it's perfect.
