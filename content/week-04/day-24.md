### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
In 2022, the most boring business in America became one of the most watched. Short videos about buying a laundromat — no staff, no inventory, customers who do the work, cash every single day — racked up hundreds of millions of views. Search interest for "buy a laundromat" spiked. Business brokers reported a wave of first-time buyers, many of them under forty, showing up with a loan pre-approval and a notes app full of numbers from a video. Three years on, those stores have new owners. Some are doing fine. Some paid far too much. This is the story of a gold rush in a strip mall: what was true, what was hype, and who actually owns the laundromat down your street now.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
For decades, nobody wanted a laundromat. It was the business your uncle had, in a strip mall, with a soda machine and a change-maker that jammed. The owners were typically older, often first-generation immigrants, and many had run the same store for twenty or thirty years. The numbers were quiet and good — we covered them in week one: around thirty thousand stores, roughly a five-billion-dollar industry, revenue per store in the low to mid six figures, no receivables, no stock, and the customer doing the labour. But quiet money doesn't get noticed. And then, almost overnight, it did.

**[1:30 — The pitch]**
Here's the pitch that went viral, and to be fair to it, most of it was true. One: the owner base is retiring. Thousands of operators were hitting their seventies with no family member who wanted the store, which meant a lot of businesses for sale at once. Two: the model is simple enough to explain in sixty seconds — pay up front, self-service, cash flow every day. Three: the money is bankable. Small-business lenders like laundromats, so a buyer with modest savings could get a government-backed loan for most of the price. Four: the famous survival statistic. The industry's own trade group has long claimed a five-year success rate around ninety-five percent, and that number got repeated in every video, usually without the words "according to the industry." Put together, the pitch was irresistible: a recession-proof, financeable, retiring-owner business that your parents' generation had overlooked. Hundreds of thousands of people decided to look.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Now the machine of the gold rush itself. Who sold? Retiring owners, and the timing was perfect for them. A store that might have taken a year to sell suddenly had multiple buyers. Who bought? Mostly individuals — first-time business owners, often with a day job, buying one store with a loan. Some built to two or three. A smaller group of local operators, people who already owned a few, bought more. And at the top, a handful of investors began stacking stores into regional groups, usually with newer equipment, card payment systems and a pickup-and-delivery service bolted on. At what price? This is where it gets interesting. Small laundromats are priced as a multiple of their profit — historically three to four times what the owner takes home. During the rush, multiples pushed toward five in hot markets. But here's the thing about the number being multiplied. In a cash business with old coin machines, "profit" is often whatever the seller says it is. No receipts, no card data, a handwritten ledger. Buyers who'd learned the model from a sixty-second video were paying five times a number nobody could verify. The best operators knew this and insisted on stores with card readers and real data. The rest paid for a story.

**[5:30 — The truth]**
So what did the videos skip? We covered the operating costs in an earlier episode — utilities eating a fifth of revenue, the equipment retool, the lease. The gold rush added three more. First, the survival statistic. That ninety-five percent is a trade-group claim with no independent study behind it; the honest answer is that nobody knows the failure rate, and a store bought at the wrong price can fail in a business that otherwise rarely does. Second, the "no employees" line. A self-service store with no attendant works in some neighbourhoods and gets vandalised in others; most well-run stores have at least part-time staff, and the growing wash-dry-fold business — which is where new owners found their best margins — is a labour business by definition. Third, the delivery dream. Pickup-and-delivery laundry became the add-on every new owner planned to launch, and it works, but it's an app, a van, a driver and marketing — a second business on top of the first. The store doesn't run itself; it runs you, a few hours a day, for a long time. And the quiet fourth truth: the people who did best were the ones who already understood small business — plumbing, leases, local marketing — and used the videos as a map, not a plan.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What does this mean for you? If you use a laundromat: the new owner probably paid more than the old one ever would have, which is why the machines got new card readers and the prices went up. If you've thought about buying one: the business was never the problem, and it still isn't. The price was. Three questions settle almost every deal — how old are the machines, what does the gas bill say, and how many years are on the lease — and none of them fit in a sixty-second video. My take: the gold rush was right that laundromats are a great boring business, and wrong about the one thing that matters in buying any business — what you pay. Viral attention is the enemy of a good price. The time to buy a boring business is when it's still boring.
