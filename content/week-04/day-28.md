### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
Somewhere in a filing cabinet is a document your family will one day sign for a few thousand dollars. It looks like a property deed. It isn't. It's a right of interment — permission to place a body in a specific piece of ground that the cemetery will own forever. You pay for the land, you never get the land, and the cemetery is legally required to look after it for the rest of time. That one sentence contains the strangest business model on this channel: real estate that is sold once, never transferred, and never finished. Here's how cemeteries make money, why the law makes them behave like endowments, and the problem that every one of them eventually runs into.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
Most people don't think of a cemetery as a business at all. It's a place, a quiet one, usually attached in our minds to a church or a town. And many cemeteries are exactly that — run by municipalities, religious groups or nonprofits. But a large share of American cemeteries are private, for-profit operations, and the biggest funeral company in the country, the one we met in an earlier episode, owns hundreds of them. The reason they're attractive is the reason they're strange: the customer base is guaranteed, the product takes up almost no space, and the asset is never given away. We think of it as sacred ground. The balance sheet thinks of it as inventory.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
The modern cemetery business was invented in the nineteenth century, when cities ran out of churchyard space and private "rural cemeteries" were laid out on the edge of town as landscaped parks. They sold family plots in advance, which gave them something churches never had: cash up front for ground that wouldn't be used for decades. Two things turned that into an industry. First, the shift to selling rights rather than land — the cemetery stays whole, nothing can ever be subdivided or built on, and the operator keeps control of every blade of grass. Second, the perpetual-care law. After too many Victorian cemeteries went bankrupt and feral, states began requiring that a slice of every sale — commonly ten to fifteen percent — be deposited into a trust whose principal could never be touched. Only the income could pay for maintenance. From that point on, a cemetery wasn't just a landowner. It was a fund with a lawn.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Now the machine, and it has three parts. Part one: density. A traditional grave takes a small rectangle of ground. An acre fits somewhere around a thousand of them. At a modest twenty-five hundred dollars a plot, that's about two and a half million dollars of sales per acre — for land that might cost a tiny fraction of that as a field. And that's before the add-ons: the opening-and-closing fee, the concrete vault or liner that most cemeteries require, the headstone and its installation, the flowers programme, the bench. A single burial can bring the cemetery and its partners well into five figures. Part two: time. The cemetery sells plots years or decades before they're used — "pre-need," the same idea the funeral homes run. The money arrives now; the cost of the burial arrives much later; and in between, the perpetual-care trust grows, quietly, on money that came from customers who are still alive. Part three: vertical inventory. When the flat ground runs low, the operator builds a mausoleum — a wall of crypts that stacks a dozen burials on the footprint of one grave — or a columbarium, a wall of small niches for cremated remains. A niche might sell for one to three thousand dollars and occupy a space the size of a shoebox. Cremation, which is eating the funeral home's margin, turns out to be the cemetery's densest product. Put the three together: guaranteed demand, land sold a thousand times per acre, revenue decades ahead of cost, and a legally mandated trust growing underneath. It's a landlord that never has to find a new tenant, because the tenants never leave. And unlike almost every other business on this channel, the price rarely gets negotiated. Families buy at the worst possible moment to comparison-shop, usually within a few miles of home, usually where relatives already are. The cemetery doesn't compete on price with the one across town; it competes on who's already buried there.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
And that is also the catch, because the tenants never leave. A cemetery has a finite number of plots and an infinite obligation. The day it sells its last space, sales revenue goes to zero — and the mowing, the fences, the drainage, the fallen headstones go on forever. That's exactly what the perpetual-care trust is for, and in a well-run cemetery that's been depositing for a century, it works. But a trust funded at ten percent of 1950s plot prices does not pay for 2020s landscaping labour. Across the country, sold-out cemeteries have slipped into neglect, and when a private operator walks away, the town often inherits the bill. The second catch is cremation's other edge: families who scatter ashes buy nothing from the cemetery at all. Burial rates have been falling for decades, which shrinks the pipeline of new plot sales even as the care obligation keeps growing. Third, regulation and reputation. Cemeteries are governed by a patchwork of state rules, and the industry has had its share of scandals — oversold sections, reused graves, trusts that were quietly raided. And finally, there's no exit. You cannot redevelop a cemetery into apartments. In most places you can't even sell the land. Whatever you built, you keep. That's why the smartest operators treat a cemetery less like a property and more like a pension fund: the real job isn't selling plots, it's making sure the trust is large enough, early enough, that the lawn still gets cut when the last plot is gone and the last customer's grandchildren have moved away.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What does this mean for you? If you're buying a plot: understand that you're buying a right, not property, and ask what the perpetual-care fund looks like, because that fund is the only thing standing between your family's plot and a field of weeds in fifty years. If you're interested in the business: this is the rare case where the moat and the trap are the same feature. My take: cemeteries are the purest expression of selling time — cash today for a promise that outlasts everyone who made it. It's a brilliant model for about a hundred years. The question is always what happens in year one hundred and one.
