### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
This morning you probably walked over a floor mat at a shop, a café, a garage. Somebody rents that mat. A truck brought it, will take it away next week, and will leave a clean one. The same truck brought the mechanic's shirt, the restaurant's towels, the soap in the restroom. One company does this for about a million businesses, makes over nine billion dollars a year doing it, and has raised its dividend every year for more than four decades. It has never been in a headline you remember. Today: how washing other people's work clothes became one of the best businesses in America, and why the thing that makes it great is also the thing customers complain about.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
The assumption, if anyone thinks about it at all, is that a business buys its own uniforms, its own mats, its own towels. Some do. But think about what that means for a restaurant with thirty staff: buying, laundering, replacing, and keeping track of a hundred shirts. Nobody wants that job. So an entire industry exists to do it for them: uniform and facility-services rental. You don't buy the shirt; you rent it, by the week, delivered clean. It sounds trivial. It's a sixteen-billion-dollar-a-year market in North America, and the largest player in it is bigger than most airlines.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
It started with rags. In 1929, in Cincinnati, a man began collecting oily rags from factories, washing them, and selling them back clean. That was the whole business: things companies were throwing away, cleaned and returned for a fee. His family kept it going through the Depression, and in the 1950s and 60s the grandson made the leap from rags to uniforms. The insight was simple. Factories needed work clothes that were clean, matching and replaced when they wore out, and they'd happily pay a weekly fee to never think about it. One rental truck could serve dozens of plants. Then came mats — logo mats at every entrance. Then shop towels, restroom supplies, first-aid cabinets, fire-extinguisher inspections. Every new product rode on the same truck to the same customers. By the time the company went public in the 1980s, the model was set: rent the boring stuff, deliver it weekly, never let the customer leave.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Here's the machine, and the whole thing is the route. Picture one driver, one truck, forty stops a day — a mechanic, a diner, a dentist, a warehouse, over and over. At each stop he swaps dirty for clean and moves on. Now add a forty-first customer, a new café between two existing stops. What does it cost to serve them? The truck was already driving past. The laundry plant has spare capacity. The driver adds four minutes. Almost all of that new customer's monthly fee drops straight to profit. That's route density, and it's why this business gets better as it gets bigger: every new stop on an existing route is cheaper to serve than the last. Then stack the products. A customer who rents uniforms also needs mats, which also means towels, which also means soap dispensers — each one a few extra dollars a week on a stop the truck was already making. The average customer ends up with four or five services and a bill it never looks at closely, because the amount is small and the hassle it replaces is large. Now put numbers on it. The biggest player runs hundreds of facilities, thousands of routes, and makes operating margins in the low twenties on a service that's essentially laundry and logistics. More than nine-tenths of its revenue recurs every week without a new sale. The whole industry sits on a few giants — the one we've described, one or two large rivals — because once a route is dense, a new entrant can't match the cost per stop. The boring product is the product. The route is the moat. And notice what the customer is really buying: not a shirt, but the absence of a problem. The restaurant owner never has to think about laundry again, and a service that removes thinking is one people keep paying for long after they've forgotten what it costs. That's why the company can raise prices a little every year and lose almost nobody.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
The catch is in the contract, and it's a real one. The standard agreement runs about five years and renews automatically unless the customer cancels in a narrow window before the end. Prices adjust upward every year. Lost or damaged garments get billed. Small businesses sign the agreement on day one — it's just mats and shirts, after all — and discover years later that they're locked in, paying more than they expected, with an exit that requires a certified letter at exactly the right time. Lawsuits and complaints about these terms are a recurring feature of the industry. That's the uncomfortable truth about the model: the recurring revenue investors love is partly the customer's difficulty in leaving. Two more catches. First, this is a logistics-and-labour business: drivers, plant workers, fuel, water, energy — every one of those costs has gone up, and a route is only efficient when it's full. Second, the customers are small businesses, and when restaurants and shops close in a downturn, stops disappear and routes get thin. The giant survived the pandemic and came out stronger, but its smaller competitors felt every empty stop. There's also the plain physical reality of the work: industrial laundries use enormous amounts of water and energy, and environmental rules on wastewater and chemicals get tighter every year. The business looks like a subscription on a spreadsheet, but it still runs on boilers, trucks and people who start at five in the morning.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What does this mean for you? If you run a business, read the rental agreement before you sign, know your cancellation window, and put the date in your calendar five years out. If you're interested in investing or in starting something: the lesson is bigger than mats. The best businesses sell a small, repeating, unglamorous convenience to customers who'd rather pay than think about it — and win by being the cheapest to deliver, not the cheapest to buy. My take: this is the most boring great business on this channel, and the most instructive. Nobody bragged about renting rags in 1929. The trucks kept rolling anyway.
