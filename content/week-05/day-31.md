### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
Pull into almost any gas station in America and look at the sign. A global oil brand, a logo you've known since childhood. Now here's the thing: that company almost certainly doesn't own the station. It doesn't own the land, the pumps, the store, or the person behind the counter. It owns the sign — or rather, it rents the right to put it there. The business underneath belongs to someone else, and it doesn't make its money the way you think. Today: who actually owns the hundred and fifty thousand places where America buys fuel, why the fuel barely pays the bills, and where the profit really comes from.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
The assumption is simple. Oil company drills oil, refines it, sells it at its own stations. Vertical integration, top to bottom. And decades ago, that was closer to the truth — the majors owned and operated thousands of stations. But the modern gas station is really two businesses stapled together: a fuel retailer and a convenience store. And the first one, the one with the famous name on it, is the worse business of the two by a wide margin. Understanding why explains everything about who owns what.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
Through the nineteen-nineties and two-thousands, the big oil companies did something that looks strange from the outside: they sold their gas stations. Thousands of them. Running a retail site — the staff, the leases, the local regulations, the underground tanks — was a low-margin headache compared with drilling and refining. So they got out of the retail business and into the branding business. Today the majors own well under one percent of the stations that carry their names. Who bought them? Three kinds of people. Independent operators — around sixty percent of convenience stores in America are single-store businesses, very often family-run. Regional distributors, called jobbers, who buy fuel wholesale, supply dozens or hundreds of sites, and often own the real estate. And chains — some of them enormous, several of them foreign-owned — that run thousands of stores. The brand on the canopy is a contract: the station agrees to buy that company's fuel and keep its standards, and in exchange gets the logo, the national advertising and the card-processing network. It's a franchise for a sign.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Now the machine, and start with the pump. A gallon of fuel sells for, say, three and a half dollars. The station's gross margin on that gallon — the gap between what it paid the supplier and what you paid — has averaged somewhere around thirty to forty cents in recent years, and historically less. Then the credit card company takes two to three percent of the whole price, not of the margin. On a three-fifty gallon that's eight to twelve cents, gone. So the station nets maybe twenty cents on a good day, and when wholesale prices spike faster than the sign can change, it can lose money on every gallon for a week. Across the industry, card fees are now so large that trade groups say they exceed total pretax profit. The fuel, in other words, is not the business. It's the reason you turn in. Now walk inside. Coffee costs cents to make and sells for two dollars. Fountain drinks, snacks, cigarettes, lottery, the roller grill, the hot case: inside the store, gross margins run around thirty percent, ten times what the pump makes. Inside sales are a smaller share of revenue, yet they generate the majority of a typical station's gross profit. That's the whole model: lose a little on the thing everyone needs, make it back on the things people grab while they're there. The best operators in the country have taken this to its logical end — stores so good that people drive past three cheaper stations to get to the one with the famous brisket or the clean bathrooms. The fuel became a loss leader for a restaurant. And the ownership follows the money: the people who own the stations are the people who understand retail, not refining.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
The catches are real and getting bigger. First, the cards. Every year more customers pay with plastic, and every swipe moves margin from the station to the bank. For a single-store owner, that's the largest controllable cost that isn't labour, and it isn't very controllable. Second, the tanks. Every station sits on underground storage tanks that are regulated, insured and, eventually, replaced — at a cost that can run into the hundreds of thousands. A leak is an environmental liability that follows the property. Third, labour and crime: a store open at two in the morning has staffing costs and risks that a normal shop never faces. And fourth, the electric question. If cars stop needing gasoline, the pump stops being the reason to turn in. The smart operators already know this, which is why the newest stations look like food halls with chargers in the car park. The brand on the sign may matter less every year. The quality of the coffee may matter more.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What does this mean for you? When you pay at the pump, the famous name is getting a licence fee, the bank is getting a bigger cut than the station, and the local owner is hoping you walk inside. If you want to support the person who actually runs the place, buy the coffee. If you're looking at the business: the station is a retail store that happens to sell fuel, and it should be judged on inside sales per square foot, not on gallons. My take: the gas station is the clearest example on this channel of a brand and a business being two different things — one owns the sign, the other does the work, and only one of them is standing behind the counter at midnight.
