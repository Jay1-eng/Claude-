### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
In June 2021, a company rang the opening bell on the New York Stock Exchange and was valued at more than four billion dollars. It didn't make software. It didn't make electric cars. It washed them. Mister Car Wash, with a few hundred locations, had just become the first car wash chain to go public — and its biggest shareholder was a private equity firm that had bought it years earlier for a fraction of that. Behind it, dozens of other investment funds were doing the same thing: buying family car washes, building shiny new ones, and rolling them into chains. Why would Wall Street fall in love with one of the most boring businesses on the planet? Because somebody figured out how to turn a car wash into a subscription.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
For most of its history, the car wash was a bad business dressed up as a simple one. You paid when it rained — meaning you didn't. Revenue swung with the weather and the season. The equipment broke constantly. Labour was heavy: people hand-drying cars, vacuuming interiors, directing traffic. And every town had three of them competing on price. Owners were typically families who'd run the same wash for thirty years, with a loyal customer base and thin margins. Banks didn't love them. Investors ignored them. Which, as we keep seeing on this channel, is often where the opportunity hides.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
Two things changed. The first was the express exterior tunnel: a conveyor pulls the car through a hundred-foot building of brushes, soap and dryers, and it comes out the other end clean in three minutes. No interior cleaning, no hand drying, almost no staff — a couple of attendants loading cars and a manager. The tunnel can wash more than a hundred cars an hour, and free vacuums out front keep customers happy without adding labour. The second change was the big one: the unlimited plan. For twenty to forty dollars a month, you get as many washes as you want. A sticker on your windshield opens the gate automatically. Suddenly a car wash wasn't selling washes. It was selling memberships. And memberships behave completely differently from washes: they don't care if it rains, they don't care about the season, and they keep paying whether you show up or not.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Let's run the numbers the way an investor would. Start with the membership. At Mister Car Wash, members now account for roughly seventy percent of wash revenue. Say a member pays twenty-five dollars a month. Industry operators report that the average member washes somewhere around twice a month. That's twelve-fifty per wash — less than the walk-up price, which sounds bad — until you realise a huge share of members wash far less than that, and the marginal cost of one more car through the tunnel is a dollar or two of water, chemicals and electricity. Members who barely come are nearly pure profit. It's the gym model, bolted onto a building full of brushes. Now the building. A modern express wash costs several million dollars to build, including land. But a good location can do tens of thousands of cars a month and generate well over a million dollars a year in revenue, with site-level margins that trade reports put at forty, fifty, sometimes sixty percent before rent and debt. Labour is the other half of the story. An old full-service wash might employ fifteen people on a Saturday — washers, dryers, cashiers, vacuum attendants. An express tunnel runs with three or four, because the customer stays in the car, the conveyor does the moving, and the free vacuums are self-service. Fewer people, fewer problems, and a cost base that barely moves when volume goes up. That combination — predictable subscription revenue, low labour, high margins, and a physical asset on a corner lot — is catnip for private equity. It also explains the valuations. A business with recurring revenue gets priced like a software company, not like a car wash; investors were reportedly paying well over ten times annual earnings for good chains at the peak. So the playbook wrote itself: buy a chain, add the unlimited plan to every location, build new tunnels on busy roads, use the subscription cash flow to borrow more, and repeat. Chains grew from dozens of sites to hundreds in a few years, and the number of express washes in America exploded.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
Then the music changed. Three problems hit at once. First, oversupply. When every fund is chasing the same corner lots, towns that supported two car washes suddenly have six, and the unlimited-plan customer only needs one. Membership growth at new sites slowed. Second, debt. Much of the building spree was financed when interest rates were near zero. When rates jumped in 2022 and 2023, the loans got expensive and the valuations fell. Several operators and lenders have reportedly run into trouble, and some brand-new washes have changed hands at a discount. Third, churn. Members sign up at a discount, realise they wash twice a year, and cancel. A model built on people forgetting their subscription has a ceiling — eventually they remember. And there's a quieter fourth problem: the wash itself got commoditised. When every tunnel on the road offers the same unlimited plan at the same price with the same free vacuums, the only thing left to compete on is location and price — and price wars are exactly what the subscription was supposed to escape. The family owner who ran a wash for thirty years never had these problems, because they never borrowed a hundred million dollars to build twenty more.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What does this mean for you? If you're a customer, the unlimited plan is a genuine bargain — if you actually wash twice a month or more. If you don't, you're the subsidy. If you're thinking about the business, understand what's really being sold: a membership, a location, and a building, in that order. The brushes are the least important part. My take: the subscription was one of the smartest ideas in boring business in the last twenty years. The land grab that followed was the same mistake investors make every cycle — a great business model does not survive building three times more of it than the town needs.
