### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
In the spring of 2020, airplanes were parked in the desert. Nobody was flying. United Airlines was burning through cash, and it needed a loan — fast. So it put up collateral. Not the planes. Not the gates. It pledged its frequent-flyer program. The bankers looked at MileagePlus and valued it at about twenty-two billion dollars. At that moment, the entire airline — planes, people, routes, everything — was worth *less* than that on the stock market. The loyalty program was worth more than the company that owned it. That is not a quirk. That is the business. Here's how airlines quietly turned into banks.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
Ask anyone what an airline sells and they'll say seats. Flights. And they do sell those. The problem is that flying is one of the worst businesses ever invented. Fuel is your biggest cost and you can't control the price. Your labor is unionized. A storm in Chicago cancels your flights in Miami. And the moment a competitor undercuts you by ten dollars, your customers leave. Warren Buffett once joked that a capitalist should have shot down Orville Wright to save investors money. Over the long run, the core business of flying people around has earned margins that would make a grocery store blush. So if flying is a terrible business… how are these companies worth tens of billions? Because flying isn't the business anymore.

**[1:30 — Origin]**
Rewind to 1981. Airlines had just been deregulated. Suddenly anybody could compete on any route, and prices collapsed. American Airlines needed a reason for customers to stay loyal that *wasn't* price. So it launched AAdvantage — the first modern frequent-flyer program. Fly with us, collect points, get a free trip. It was a loyalty gimmick. Then someone had a much bigger idea. What if other companies wanted to give away miles too? Hotels. Rental cars. And above all — credit cards. Banks were desperate for a reward that felt exciting, and a free flight to Hawaii feels a lot more exciting than two percent cash back. So airlines started *selling* miles to banks. By the wholesale ton. The gimmick became a product.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Here's the machine, step by step. Step one: the airline creates miles. Cost to create a mile: essentially zero. It's a number in a database. Step two: it sells those miles to a bank — American Express, Chase, Citi — for somewhere around one to two cents each. Step three: the bank gives you those miles for spending on its card, and the bank earns interchange fees and interest from you. Step four: eventually, you redeem. And here's the beautiful part for the airline. The seat you get is often one that would have flown empty. The cost of putting you in it is a bag of pretzels and a little fuel. Now put real numbers on it. In 2023, American Express alone paid Delta roughly six point eight billion dollars. Delta's management has talked openly about pushing that toward ten billion a year. That's more than a tenth of everything Delta brings in — and it arrives with margins that flying can only dream of. And there's a bonus: miles are a liability on the balance sheet, but a lot of them never get redeemed at all. Expired, forgotten, orphaned in an old account. Accountants call that breakage. To an airline, it's free money. Then 2020 proved the point in public. United borrowed around six point eight billion dollars against MileagePlus. American raised about ten billion against AAdvantage. Delta borrowed about nine billion against SkyMiles. The planes were grounded, and the banks still lent — because the loyalty programs kept earning even while nobody flew.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
So who pays for all this? Mostly you — in two ways. First, the miles you earn are worth less every year. Airlines moved to "dynamic" award pricing, which is a polite way of saying the flight that cost twenty-five thousand miles now costs sixty thousand. They can devalue the currency whenever they like, because they *are* the central bank. Second, the people who fund the whole thing are cardholders who carry a balance. The bank's interest income is what makes those big payments to Delta possible. The guy paying twenty-four percent on his card is effectively buying someone else's first-class upgrade. In 2024, the U.S. Department of Transportation opened an inquiry into airline loyalty programs for exactly these reasons — devaluations, hidden fees, and how miles are valued. Whether anything changes is still an open question.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
Here's what this means for you. If you fly a lot and pay your card in full every month, you are on the winning side of the machine: you're getting a subsidy from the people who don't. If you carry a balance to chase miles, you are the subsidy. And the next time an airline says it's "struggling," look at its loyalty revenue before you feel sorry for it. My take: this is genuinely brilliant business design — and it's also a currency with no rules, run by the people who profit from printing it. Both of those things are true.

**[7:40 — Bridge]**
Airlines aren't the only company that figured out the real money is in the thing *around* the product. McDonald's did it with land. That's tomorrow's Short — and the full story is next week. Subscribe so you catch it.
