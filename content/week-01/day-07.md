### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
A porta-potty costs about a thousand dollars. It rents for roughly a hundred a month. It can last ten years. Do the maths and you get a plastic box that pays for itself in a year and then earns for a decade — and nobody at your high school reunion will ever want to hear about it. That's the point. Today we're looking at three businesses most people would never touch: portable toilets, septic pumping and dumpster rental. They are dirty, unglamorous and, by most accounts, some of the most reliable money in small business. The reason is a pattern I'm going to call the disgust moat.

**[0:35 — The assumption]**
When people imagine starting a business they imagine something they'd be proud to describe. A café. A clothing brand. An app. Those businesses have a problem: everybody wants them, so everybody starts them, so margins get competed down to nothing. The businesses in this video have the opposite problem. The work is smelly, physical and unfashionable. The trucks are expensive. The customers are construction foremen, landlords and homeowners with a flooded yard. There's no prestige — which means there's very little competition. And in business, competition is the thing that eats your profit. So the lack of it isn't a bug. It's the entire opportunity.

**[1:30 — Three businesses, one pattern]**
Business one: portable toilets. A new unit, around a thousand dollars. Rental to a construction site, roughly a hundred a month, plus weekly servicing. Demand isn't optional: US workplace safety rules require a toilet for roughly every twenty workers on a job site. Add festivals, weddings and marathons, and the same units get hosed out and sent to the next job. Business two: septic pumping. About one in five American homes isn't on a city sewer — it has a septic tank, and that tank needs pumping every three to five years whether the owner likes it or not. A pump-out typically costs the homeowner three to six hundred dollars and takes an hour. The truck — a vacuum tank on a chassis — runs well into six figures, which is the main reason the guy with the truck gets to charge what he charges. Business three: dumpster rental. A roll-off container costs four to six thousand dollars of steel. Deliver it to a renovation or a clean-out, pick it up a week later, charge three to six hundred dollars per haul, and send it straight back out. One truck can run a fleet of twenty boxes. Three businesses. Same shape: a durable, boring asset, rented repeatedly, to customers who have no choice.

**[3:00 — The machine]**
Here's why the money is so clean. First, the demand is legally or physically mandatory. Nobody rents a porta-potty for fun; the law says the site needs one. Nobody pumps a septic tank for fun; the alternative is sewage in the garden. Mandatory demand means customers don't shop around much and don't cancel in a downturn. Second, the asset is simple and lasts forever. A plastic toilet or a steel box has no software, no fashion cycle and almost no depreciation beyond scratches. Every dollar after payback is mostly profit, minus the truck, the driver and disposal fees. Third — and this is the moat — the disgust. The work filters out most would-be competitors before they start. The ones who do start need a six-figure truck, disposal permits and a strong stomach. That combination keeps local markets small: a town often has two or three operators, who've been there twenty years, who know every contractor, and who quietly raise prices with inflation every year. Compare that to pressure washing, where the barrier to entry is a two-thousand-dollar machine and the competition is every teenager with a trailer. Low disgust, low moat, low prices. High disgust, high moat, high prices. The smell is doing the work that a patent does in other industries. And because the customers are businesses rather than consumers, the pricing is quietly generous. A contractor bidding a six-month job doesn't argue over whether the toilet costs ninety or a hundred and ten a month; it's a rounding error on the project, so it gets approved and forgotten. That's the sweet spot: a cost that's mandatory for the customer, trivial to the customer, and highly profitable to you.

**[5:30 — The catch]**
It isn't free money, and the catches are real. One: routes. All three businesses are logistics companies in disguise. The profit lives in how many stops a truck can make in a day; a badly planned route or a far-flung customer eats the margin in fuel and hours. Two: the truck. A vacuum truck or a roll-off hauler can cost a hundred to two hundred thousand dollars, and when it breaks, revenue stops. Three: disposal. You can't just dump it. Waste goes to licensed treatment plants and landfills that charge by the ton or gallon, and those fees rise. Four: people. Finding drivers who'll do this work reliably is the single biggest complaint from owners — and it's why the businesses sell for good money to the few operators who've solved it. And finally, consolidation: the big waste companies and private-equity-backed roll-ups have noticed, and in some markets they're buying the family operators and raising prices. There's one more catch that's easy to miss: these businesses are local by nature. A dumpster or a toilet only earns within an hour's drive of the yard, so growth means buying more trucks and more yards, not just adding customers. That's why the operators who do best tend to pick one county and own it, rather than trying to be everywhere. The moat protects whoever's inside it — which may not be you.

**[7:00 — Payoff + opinion]**
What should you take from this? If you're a customer, understand you're paying for scarcity, not for the toilet. If you're thinking about business, the lesson is bigger than toilets: the best small businesses are often the ones that are hard to *want*, not hard to do. Look for mandatory demand, a simple asset and a barrier that's emotional rather than technical. My take: in a world where everyone's chasing the same ten glamorous ideas, disgust might be the last honest moat left.
