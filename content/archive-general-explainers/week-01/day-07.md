### Script

**[0:00 — Cold open]**
Imagine a store that charges you sixty-five dollars a year before it will let you buy anything. And then imagine that store is one of the most loved companies in America, with a renewal rate above ninety percent. That store is Costco, and the membership fee isn't a side business. It's basically *the* business. Over the last decade, almost every major company has figured out the same trick: don't just sell a product. Sell the *right* to buy the product. Costco. Amazon Prime. Your gym. Your streaming apps. This is the membership trap — and once you see the machine, you'll notice it everywhere.

**[0:40 — Costco]**
Start with the warehouse. Costco sells a lot of stuff — over two hundred billion dollars' worth a year. But its margins on that stuff are famously thin. It caps markups at around fourteen percent on most products, and lower on its own Kirkland brand. Walmart marks up more than that. So how is Costco so profitable? Membership fees. Those fees bring in well over four billion dollars a year, and because there's almost no cost attached to them, they flow nearly straight to profit. Depending on the year, that's half or more of Costco's operating income. Read that again: the cards make more profit than the warehouses. The four-ninety-nine chicken, the one-fifty hot dog, the gas station out front — none of them need to make money. Their job is to make you feel like the card pays for itself. And it works. More than nine out of ten members renew. In 2024 Costco raised the fee for the first time in seven years, and almost nobody left.

**[2:10 — Amazon Prime]**
Now take the same idea and remove the building. Amazon Prime launched in 2005 as free two-day shipping for seventy-nine dollars a year. Today it's a hundred and thirty-nine, and Amazon's "subscription services" line — which is mostly Prime — brings in over forty billion dollars a year. But the fee isn't even the point. The point is what the fee does to your behaviour. Once you've paid for shipping, every purchase anywhere else feels like a waste. Prime members spend far more per year than non-members — the estimates vary, but roughly double is a common one. You've prepaid, so you want to "get your money's worth," and the only way to do that is to buy more. Amazon turned a shipping fee into a psychological fence around its store. And then it kept adding things inside the fence — video, music, grocery delivery — not because each one makes money, but because every one makes cancelling feel harder.

**[3:50 — Gyms]**
Here's the strange one. Costco and Amazon want you to use the membership more. Gyms want you to use it *less*. A budget gym signs up thousands of members — think six or seven thousand for a single location — in a building that can hold a few hundred people at once. The model only works because most people don't come. So the gym makes it easy to join, cheap enough that cancelling feels like a hassle, and friendly enough that you don't feel bad about not showing up. Free pizza nights. "No judgement" branding. An annual fee that quietly hits once a year. Same trap, opposite goal: you pay for access, and the business profits from how little of it you take.

**[5:20 — The psychology]**
Why does this work on all of us? Three things. One: the sunk-cost effect. Once you've paid, your brain wants to justify it, so you shop more, or you keep the gym "just in case." Two: loss aversion. Cancelling feels like *losing* something — the shipping, the discounts, the option — even if you weren't using it. Three: the mental accounting trick. The fee is paid once, in the past, so every individual purchase feels cheaper than it is. There's no shipping fee on this order. The chicken is only five bucks. The gym is "already paid for." Companies know these three effects better than you do. That's why everything you touch is now a "membership," a "subscription," or a "program." The product is the same. The relationship changed.

**[6:40 — What to do]**
So what do you do about it? Split your memberships into two groups. Group one: pay-to-*save*. Costco, if you actually shop there. Prime, if you actually order weekly. These are deals, but only if you use them, so do the maths once a year: fee divided by how much you really saved. Group two: pay-and-*forget*. The gym you haven't visited since January. The streaming app you opened twice. These aren't memberships. They're donations with extra steps. Cancel them today — not in the new year, today — and watch how hard they make it. That difficulty tells you everything about which side of the trap you're on. My take: membership models are some of the smartest business design of the last twenty years. They're also the most honest, in a way — the price of the trap is printed right on the card. You just have to read it.
